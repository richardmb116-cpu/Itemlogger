# Obsolescence, not depreciation, should set the sell date

The evidence says Keep Track's upgrade planner is built on the right idea, comparing next year's loss with the average yearly cost, but on the wrong curves and missing the cost term that actually justifies selling. Second-hand value does not follow one shape. It follows four. **Fast-decaying tech** loses 15–50% in year one depending on brand (iPhones keep about 80–87% of price on private marketplaces within the first year, while Galaxy S phones keep about 68%) ([BGR](https://bgr.com/2215334/do-samsung-depreciate-faster-iphone/)). **Durable goods** lose 30–55% on day one and then slide slowly. **Brand-locked or melt-priced goods** (lenses, Herman Miller, Rolex, Hermès, gold) barely decline with age at all. **Hygiene or fashion goods** (mattresses, fast fashion) are close to worthless once used. Economically, depreciation alone never justifies selling, because the cost of keeping an item falls as it ages. A sell date only makes sense once you count obsolescence, rising repair and failure risk, and the effort of selling ([Chowdhury, RPI replacement-analysis notes](https://arshadzahangirchowdhury.github.io/courses/engr4760-notes/06-replacement-analysis.html); [arXiv 1210.3678](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1210.3678)). The app's current answer comes almost entirely from its assumed end-of-life cliff at 60% of useful life. That cliff also pushes every item, including lenses and Rolexes, down to about 2–6% of price, beneath its own value floor. The cost curve is flat near the optimum: missing the ideal time by 20–25% costs only about 2–4% of yearly ownership cost. So the planner should show a **window**, priced in money ("selling 6 months late costs about $X"), and should run on sub-category curves fitted to the measured data in this report. Treat those curves as priors to be corrected by prices the user checks.

## Resale curves fall into four shapes, and the app models one

The firmest numbers come from smartphones, cameras, bikes, cars and luxury goods. They are the best data the research found, and they still show the problem. **iPhones lose 25–35% within 5–9 months on buyback prices, while Galaxy S phones lose 47–58%** ([SellCell](https://sellcell.com/blog/samsung-ai-phones-close-resale-gap-iphone-2026/)). On a private marketplace the same phones lose far less: a 256GB iPhone 17 kept about 87.5% of its starting price and a Galaxy S26 about 68% ([BGR](https://bgr.com/2215334/do-samsung-depreciate-faster-iphone/)). The "brand gap" itself moves around. It narrows to almost nothing when you compare unlocked units only, with the S26 at about 89% against the iPhone at 99% ([BGR](https://bgr.com/2212669/smartphone-best-resale-value)). Price changes on new models distort the ratios too. Apple's increase of the iPhone 17 starting price to $899 makes used prices look stronger when measured against the new list price ([Swappa](https://swappa.com/en/guide/apple-iphone-17)). Two lessons follow for the app. First, the selling channel (buyback, trade-in or private sale) matters as much as the brand. Second, curves should be anchored to **what the user paid**, not to list price.

Cameras show the clearest split between items that hold value and items that decay. In a six-month study of a Japanese used marketplace, **lenses sold at 66.9% of original retail and constant-aperture f/2.8 zooms at 77.7%. Mirrorless bodies sold at 60.3%, and DSLR bodies, a platform the makers have discontinued, at 35.2%** ([PhotographyTalk/Minna Camera](https://www.photographytalk.com/do-camera-lenses-hold-their-value-better-than-camera-bodies-a-data-backed-buyers-guide/)). Headphones decay steadily rather than falling off a cliff. Sony WH-1000XM4 and XM5 sets still sell at about 39–41% of launch price three to six years on, and AirPods Pro 2 at about 45% ([Swappa](https://swappa.com/blog/used-airpods-headphones-guide/)). Current-generation consoles are unusual because new prices have risen. Used Xbox Series X units sell at about $541 against $799.99 new, and PS5 Slim at about $503 against $649.99 ([Swappa](https://swappa.com/blog/used-game-consoles-complete-guide)). Last-generation hardware still has an absolute floor: a 1TB PS4 Slim "hovers just above $100" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/switch-2-ps5-used-prices-xbox-series-x-s-preowned-2000614748)).

Durable goods follow a "big drop, then slow slide" pattern, and their best data is old or comes from platforms that profit from the sale. **Mountain bikes lost about 45% in year one, 52% by year two and 59% by year three** in a measured sample of eBay auctions. Trek, Specialized, Giant and Santa Cruz differed by only 44–46% ([Singletracks](https://www.singletracks.com/mtb-gear/new-mountain-bikes-lose-about-45-of-their-value-after-year-one/)). Condition matters a great deal: Bicycle Blue Book puts the year-one loss at about 38% for "excellent" and about 62% for "fair". E-bikes fall further, to about **15–30% of new price by year five**, according to an Upway table whose method is undisclosed ([Upway](https://upway.co/blogs/news/annual-electric-bike-depreciation-rate)). A replacement Bosch battery costs $629–$797 ([ebicycles.com](https://ebicycles.com/?p=9150)), which can equal most of an older bike's resale value. For major appliances, there is **no rigorous published study of used prices**. Rules of thumb put them at 20–50% of retail ([Home Essentials Guide](https://homeessentialsguide.pages.dev/how-much-to-sell-used-kitchen-appliances-for)), and Consumer Reports says older units can sell at up to 75% off ([Yahoo/Consumer Reports](https://news.yahoo.com/used-appliances-buy-one-144437620.html)). IKEA's buyback offers, paid as store credit, form a hard floor for flat-pack furniture: **45% of the original price for "as new", 35% for "very good" and 25% for "well-used"** ([IKEA UK](https://www.ikea.com/gb/en/customer-service/knowledge/articles/719db9e4-4d8e-460f-9d31-7224188dc863.html)).

Luxury and melt-priced goods hardly decline with age, but their value swings with markets. Rebag's 2025 report puts **Hermès at 138% retention and Goyard at 132%**, while the Chanel Classic Flap has slipped to 77% of retail from 83% in 2019 ([RetailBoss/Rebag](https://retailboss.co/hermes-resale-values); [FashionBI](https://www.fashionbi.com/insights/luxury-bag-price-hikes-prestige-strategy-or-consumer-risk)). Gold sells on melt value: pawn shops pay 40–60% of melt, local buyers 60–75% and refiners 80–95% ([APMEX](https://learn.apmex.com/investing-guide/selling-gold-and-diamonds-how-value-is-really-determined/)). Watch prices have crashed before. The WatchCharts index fell about 37% from its March 2022 peak ([Quill & Pad](https://quillandpad.com/2025/02/12/watchcharts-february-2025-secondary-watch-market-update/)). Natural diamond prices fell 23% in 2024 and 9.9% in 2025 (1 carat), and lab-grown wholesale prices fell 26% in 2025 ([Rapaport via GJEPC](https://gjepc.org/solitaire/diamond-price-decline-slows-as-small-goods-stabilise-rapaport/); [JCK](https://www.jckonline.com/editorial-article/lab-grown-diamond-prices-show-signs-of-stabilizing/)). Cars calibrate the whole picture. KBB residuals are **84% after one year, 61% after three and 45% after five** ([KBB](https://www.kbb.com/car-depreciation)), and iSeeCars measured 41.8% average five-year depreciation across 950,000 sales ([TFLcar/iSeeCars](https://tflcar.com/2026/03/used-car-depreciation-five-years-iseecars-study/)). Cars are the one category where the curve is both measured and multi-year. They confirm a front-loaded drop that then flattens, and no cliff from age alone.

## Life ends at events, not birthdays

Useful life in the data is set less by age than by **events**: the end of OS support, battery wear, a platform being discontinued, or the first expensive repair. Apple's iPhone 11 (2019) is the oldest model still getting new iOS versions, so iPhones get about seven years of major OS support ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/675021/how-long-does-apple-support-iphones.html)). Batteries are a resale cliff. iPhone 15 and later are rated to keep 80% capacity for 1,000 cycles, against 500 for earlier models ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2024/02/20/apple-raises-iphone-15-advertised-battery-lifespan/)). A buyback firm says the difference between 79% and 81% battery health "can mean hundreds of dollars", and that an $89 battery replacement added about $150 to one iPhone's resale value ([ecoATM](https://blog.ecoatm.com/does-your-iphone-battery-health-percentage-hurt-its-value/)). Owners actually replace phones later than the "two-year upgrade" story suggests. Counterpoint says global replacement cycles are approaching four years ([Counterpoint](https://counterpointresearch.com/en/reports/memory-crisis-pushes-global-smartphone%20replacement-cycles-toward-4-years)). US iPhones averaged 3.84 years at replacement in 2025, up from 3.27 years in 2020 ([Powerdrill/Assurant](https://powerdrill.ai/blog/people-are-keeping-their-iphones-longer-than-ever)).

Appliances wear out, but the hazard comes from repair surveys, not resale data. Large household appliances in four EU countries averaged **12.5 years of life in 2023**. Cordless stick vacuums averaged only about 4.5 years, against about 10 years for corded vacuums, while espresso machines lasted 7.2 years and pod machines about 5.5. Most appliances were replaced for reliability reasons, whereas high-tech goods were replaced because they were out of date ([EEA](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/circularity/sectoral-modules/product-lifespans/evolution-in-average-lifespans-of-household-appliances)). Roughly **a third of refrigerators need a repair by year five** ([CR via BGR](https://bgr.com/2115888/most-reliable-refrigerator-brand-consumer-reports)). Service rates in the first year range from 3.7% (Speed Queen) to 9.2% (GE) across 33,190 calls ([Yale Appliance](https://blog.yaleappliance.com/bid/86332/The-Least-Serviced-Most-Reliable-Appliance-Brands-Reviews-Ratings)). Repair visits cost $100–$450, and the call-out fee alone is $75–$125 ([Angi](https://www.angi.com/nearme/appliance-repair); [This Old House](https://www.thisoldhouse.com/home-finances/appliance-repair-statistics)). That makes professional repair uneconomic for any appliance under about $150. Dyson covers cordless machines and their batteries for 2 years ([Dyson AU](https://dyson.com.au/support/replacement-batteries)), and a genuine replacement battery costs about $70–$150 ([SmartHomeStarter](https://smarthomestarter.com/dyson-vacuum-battery-guide-everything-you-need-to-know)). For cars, the measured end-of-life signal is a **31% jump in annual repair costs at year five and a 59% jump at year eight** ([Fool/Bumper](https://www.fool.com/money/insurance/auto/articles/how-long-should-you-keep-a-car-to-get-the-most-for-your-money)).

Brand effects are real, but narrower and less stable than the app assumes. A reputation for reliability does not guarantee a resale premium: enthusiasts note low used prices for Speed Queen washers despite their reliability ([automaticwasher.org](https://automaticwasher.org/threads/low-resale-value-on-used-speed-queen-washers-and-dryers.72361/post-956385)). Among the major mountain-bike brands, brand made almost no difference ([Singletracks](https://www.singletracks.com/mtb-gear/new-mountain-bikes-lose-about-45-of-their-value-after-year-one/)). Herman Miller and Steelcase warranties are **not transferable**, so a used chair's value comes from the refurbishment and parts market, not from the 12-year warranty ([Herman Miller](https://hermanmiller.com/warranty-terms); [Steelcase](https://steelcase.com/resources/documents/faq-warranty)). In June 2026 Herman Miller launched a factory Aeron Refurbished programme, which may eventually provide an official benchmark price ([MillerKnoll](https://news.millerknoll.com/2026-06-22-Herman-Miller-introduces-Aeron-Refurbished,-extending-the-life-of-its-most-recognized-design)).

The cost of selling is often the biggest single deduction from resale value. **eBay US charges 13.6% plus $0.40 per order** on most categories ([eBay](https://www.ebay.com/help/selling/fees-credits-invoices/selling-fees?id=4822)). Since late April 2026, **eBay Australia private sellers under AU$25,000 a year pay no final value fee**; buyers pay a protection fee instead ([Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/ebay-australia-fee-free-selling/)). Facebook Marketplace is free for local pickup and charges 10% on shipped sales ([Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/facebook-marketplace-doubling-selling-fees/)). Swappa takes 3% plus payment processing ([Swappa](https://www.swappa.com/about/fees)). Gumtree AU is free to list in most categories ([Gumtree](https://help.gumtree.com.au/AU/articles/en_US/KB_Article/Which-categories-have-a-listing-fee-AU)). Trade-in programmes pay about **60–75% of a private sale**, based only on vendor commentary ([Swappa](https://swappa.com/blog/apple-trade-in-vs-sell)). Consignment can halve luxury retention: The RealReal pays 40% on items up to $145 ([TRR FAQ](https://www.therealreal.com/faq/consign)), and Peloton's resale programme lets sellers keep 70% ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/peloton-launching-resale-market-used-bikes-treadmills-rcna210631)). The asking price is also not the sale price. Across 88.4 million eBay Best Offer listings, **sales closed at 83% of list on average, 73% when buyers bargained, and almost 80% of listings never got an offer and did not sell** ([Backus et al., NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24306.pdf)).

## Depreciation alone says never sell

In plain English: owning anything costs you a stream of money. There is the value it loses, the repairs it needs, the cost of living with an older version, and the chance it breaks before you sell it. The best time to replace is the holding period that makes the **average cost per year of the whole cycle** as low as possible. Engineers call this the "economic service life", and the average figure, adjusted for interest, the **equivalent annual cost** ([Chowdhury](https://arshadzahangirchowdhury.github.io/courses/engr4760-notes/06-replacement-analysis.html); [Wikipedia: EAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalent_annual_cost)). The textbook test asks whether next year's extra cost (the *marginal* cost) is above or below that best average. If keeping it one more year costs more than a typical year of a fresh replacement, sell now. Otherwise keep it and check again. The app's rule ("sell when next year's value loss first exceeds the average yearly cost so far") is a correct, undiscounted version of this test, **but only when marginal cost is rising**.

Depreciation-only costs do not rise. Resale value falls fastest when an item is new, so each extra year of ownership costs less in lost value than the one before. With nothing else counted, the cheapest plan is always to keep the item until it dies. Running the app's own phone curve shows this. For a $1,000 phone, the average yearly loss is $299 at 18 months, $230 at 2.5 years, $193 at 5 years and $99 at 10 years (computed in the research notes by reproducing `fractionAt`). Consumer Reports found the same for cars. Keeping a Honda Civic for 15 years instead of replacing it every five saved about **$30,800** ([CNN Money/CR](https://money.cnn.com/2007/08/30/autos/cr_drive_200k/index.htm)). The classical analysis states the reason formally: an interior optimum exists only when costs per year rise with age ([arXiv 1210.3678](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1210.3678)).

Three real forces make costs rise with age. Each can be added as a monthly cost.

**Obsolescence** is the gap between what your old item does and what a new one would do. That includes a slower phone, missing features, ending software support, and an older fridge's higher power bill. Economists call it the "inferiority gradient". If that gap grows by `g` dollars per year, every year, and resale is ignored, the best life is `T* = √(2P/g)`. Take a $1,000 item whose shortfall grows by $60 a year: it should be kept about 5.8 years. Resale value shortens that, because selling earlier gets more back. The research found **no source that measures g for any consumer category**, so the values used here are assumptions (about 6% of price per year per year for phones, 4% for laptops, 1% for vacuums, roughly zero for furniture and tools). The direction is well supported, though. Quality improvements drive PC replacement, and a short-sighted model underestimates replacement cycles by about a year ([Gordon 2009](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.1080.0448)). High-tech goods are replaced because they are out of date, not because they break ([EEA](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/publications/product-lifespans-monitoring-trends)). For appliances, the efficiency part of obsolescence is measurable. Replacing a 20-year-old fridge saves about **$150 a year** in electricity ([CERTs](https://www.cleanenergyresourceteams.org/your-old-refrigerator-energy-hog)), which pays back in 8–15 years. Energy savings are therefore a tiebreaker, not a reason to sell on their own.

**Failure risk** is the chance it breaks before you sell. If it dies, you lose its remaining resale value plus any "emergency cost", such as buying in a hurry at a bad price, downtime or lost data. Wear-out failure is commonly modelled with a Weibull curve, where a shape value above 1 means the failure risk rises with age ([NIST](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/apr/section1/apr162.htm)). LBNL fitted a shape of about 2.4 to residential appliance stock data ([LBNL](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/7z222821)). Phone failures are dominated by accidents: 18.1% of iPhone owners had an accident within two years in SquareTrade's 2010 sample ([SquareTrade](https://www.squaretrade.com/htm/pdf/iPhone_failure_rates_6_22_2010.pdf)), and accidents strike at a roughly constant rate. One counter-intuitive result is that **failure risk alone does not justify selling early** if a breakdown costs nothing extra, because the item would have reached the end of its cycle anyway. Replacing before failure pays only when there is a real emergency cost *and* the failure risk rises with age.

**Transaction costs** push the other way. A percentage fee works like a bigger first-year drop. A fixed cost per sale (postage, the hours spent listing and meeting buyers) is spread over each cycle, so it favours fewer and later sales. In the notes' worked examples, a 13% fee plus $30 raised the cost of selling at the app's date by **$25–55 a year** but barely moved the optimum once obsolescence was included.

**Discounting** (money now is worth more than money later) is second-order here. At 5% a year, it adds about 5% of current resale value to the yearly cost of holding. In every worked example it moved the optimum by only a few months.

**Sunk costs** need explicit handling. The app's benchmark, `(price − value) / years`, includes the drop the owner suffered on day one. That money is gone and should not affect a forward-looking decision. The correct benchmark is the **replacement's own best average cost** (engineers call the current item the "defender" and the replacement the "challenger"): keep the current item while its marginal cost stays below that ([Chowdhury](https://arshadzahangirchowdhury.github.io/courses/engr4760-notes/06-replacement-analysis.html)). The two benchmarks agree only if the item was bought new at full price and will be replaced like for like. They diverge for items bought on sale, bought used, received as gifts, or replaced at a different price. Consumer Reports' car-repair heuristic is the same test in practical form: divide the repair bill by the months you will keep the car, and repair if the result is below a new-car payment ([Cars.com](https://www.cars.com/articles/how-to-save-money-on-a-car-wait-1420663307170)).

Two further results shape how the app should present its answer. First, **the optimum is flat**. In the pure obsolescence case, being off by a factor x raises yearly cost by `(x + 1/x)/2`. That is +0.6% at 10% early, +2.5% at 20% early and +25% at half the optimal time. Second, once obsolescence is included, the app's current dates turn out roughly right, but for the wrong reason. In the notes' worked examples with obsolescence, the true optimum was **2.5 years for a $1,000 phone** (5% band 2.0–2.8 years), **about 3.1 years for a $3,000 laptop** and **about 4.9 years for a $1,200 Dyson**. The app's dates (2.17, 3.0 and 4.58 years) cost only $2–9 a year more than those optima. Without an obsolescence term, the same dates cost $60–380 a year more than running the item to failure. Those dates come from the cliff placed at 60% of useful life: moving the cliff start from 0.5 to 0.8 shifts the phone's date from 2.0 to 2.7 years. In other words, the model's least evidenced parameter is the one deciding the answer.

## Owners ask double, and small frictions decide whether they sell at all

The behavioural evidence explains why people miss the window even when it is right. In the classic mug experiment, owners demanded a median of about $7 to sell a mug that non-owners would pay only about $3 for, and **selling prices ran roughly twice buying prices** ([Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1990](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:v:98:y:1990:i:6:p:1325-48); [KKT 1991](https://www.fermatslibrary.com/s/the-endowment-effect-loss-aversion-and-status-quo-bias)). Meta-analyses find this endowment gap smaller for ordinary market goods, but still present ([Horowitz & McConnell 2002](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/703853.htm); [Tunçel & Hammitt 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jeeman:v:68:y:2014:i:1:p:175-187)). Status-quo bias, the tendency to stick with whatever you have, is strongest when preferences are weak and options are many ([Samuelson & Zeckhauser 1988](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/rzeckhauser/files/status_quo_bias_in_decision_making.pdf)). That describes a drawer of old gadgets well. The pile is large. Australian households report about 21 sellable unused items worth roughly **AU$6,964** ([Savings.com.au/Gumtree](https://www.savings.com.au/news/gumtree-pre-loved-items-worth-small-fortune)), and US households 36 items worth US$3,675 ([eBay Recommerce Report 2020](https://static.ebayinc.com/assets/Uploads/Documents/eBay-Recommerce-Report-2020.pdf)). Both are vendor surveys of *claimed* value. After a typical 73–83% ask-to-sale ratio and the share of listings that never sell, the realisable amount is probably about half (inferred).

The nudge evidence calls for modest expectations and an emphasis on reducing friction. In 126 government nudge-unit trials covering 23 million people, the average effect was **+1.4 percentage points (8% relative)**, against +8.7 points in published academic studies. About 70% of that gap came from publication selection ([DellaVigna & Linos](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/347454)). A widely cited meta-analytic effect size of d ≈ 0.43 shrinks to 0.08–0.31, or near zero, after correcting for publication bias ([PNAS critique](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9351519/)). The design implications follow from this.

First, show a **market value net of fees and bargaining** next to whatever the owner believes the item is worth. This counters the doubled reservation price.

Second, frame waiting as a loss in money per month. Make the forward-looking comparison ("worth $520 now, about $430 next spring") rather than the backward one ("you paid $1,200"). This defuses sunk-cost reasoning. Loss framing applied to resale specifically has no field study behind it, so it is reasoned from prospect theory, not measured.

Third, use defaults and pre-commitment. Set an expected sell window when the item is added and schedule reminders for it.

Fourth, cut friction. Prefill the listing, nudge a price cut after one to two weeks without offers (given how many eBay listings never get one), and route items under about $30–50 to donation, bundling or trade-in. That threshold is inferred from one to two hours of seller time per item.

Showing projected resale at the time of purchase is also justified: 60% of US shoppers say resale value is a key factor when they buy new ([Retail Brew/ThredUp](https://www.retailbrew.com/stories/2026/04/01/resale-is-taking-a-measurable-share-from-new-retail-thredup-reports)).

## The rule the app should run: minimise expected cost, then show a priced window

The decision rule should replace the first-crossing test with a direct monthly minimisation of the expected average cost of one ownership cycle. This is the standard renewal-reward and EAC formulation, assembled in the research notes from the classical sources ([Chowdhury](https://arshadzahangirchowdhury.github.io/courses/engr4760-notes/06-replacement-analysis.html); [arXiv 1210.3678](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1210.3678); [Rust 1987](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecm:emetrp:v:55:y:1987:i:5:p:999-1033)):

```
// monthly grid, t in years, Δ = 1/12, r = ln(1 + discountRate)
N(t)  = max(0, sellThrough · (1 − fee) · V(t) − fixedSaleCost)     // net proceeds
S(t)  = exp(−(t/η)^β)                                              // survival; η ≈ 1.1·L
h(t)  = (β/η)·(t/η)^(β−1)                                          // failure hazard
u(t)  = g·P·t                                                      // obsolescence shortfall
c(t)  = expected running/repair cost rate (optional)
num(T) = P + Σ_{t<T} S(t)·(c(t) + u(t) + h(t)·c_f)·Δ·e^{−rt}  −  S(T)·N(T)·e^{−rT}
den(T) = Σ_{t<T} S(t)·Δ·e^{−rt}
A(T)   = num(T) / den(T);   T* = argmin_{T ≥ 1 yr} A(T)
window = { T : A(T) ≤ 1.05 · A(T*) }
```

The default inputs are the parameter tables above for `V(t)`. The fee and `sellThrough` come from the region and channel: about 0.86 for eBay US, about 1.0 for local or eBay AU private sales, and 0.60–0.75 of a private sale for trade-in. Multiply by about 0.8 to convert an asking price into a sale price, using the eBay Best Offer evidence. Use Weibull β ≈ 2 for electronics (≈1.5 where accidents dominate) and ≈2.5 for appliances, with the obsolescence gradient `g` by category as above. **Every one of `g`, `c_f`, η and β is an assumption, not a measured value**, and the app should label them that way.

Three special cases need explicit outcomes. If `A(T)` is still falling at 1.5 times useful life, or the net proceeds at T* are below the fixed cost of selling, the status is **"use it up"**. If the category has no age-driven decline (gold, watches, designer bags, designer furniture), the status is **"hold; price follows the market"**, and the app should not give a date at all. If the user has a checked price, or bought the item used, at a discount or as a gift, use the forward-looking test: keep while this month's marginal cost `c + u + r·N − N′ + h·(N + c_f)` stays below the replacement's own best `A*`.

Events then position the user inside the window rather than defining it. List two to four weeks before a known successor announcement (September for iPhone, January or February for Galaxy S). Sell before the battery health estimate crosses 80%, before an OS or Chromebook update cut-off, and before a camera mount or tool battery platform is discontinued. List seasonal gear one to two months before its season, shifting the months by six for Australia. Hold classic luxury goods until after a retail price increase.

The presentation should be in money and in ranges. The flatness result means "best date ± 6 months" is a false precision in one direction and too narrow in the other. A good label is "Sell between May and October next year. Selling a year later costs about $40". It shows the cost of being early or late, which in the notes' examples was asymmetric for phones: being 12 months early cost $56 a year, against $23 a year for being 12 months late. Value estimates should be shown with honest ranges, widened where the table above says the basis is inferred.

## Twelve changes to lifecycle.ts and upgrade.ts

Running the app's current `fractionAt` and crossing rule (good condition, no warranty bonus, no calibration) against the evidence shows where it breaks. The **end-of-life cliff multiplies the value after the floor has been applied**, so every item converges to about 15% of an already-small working value. Over-ear headphones fall to 6% of price at year four against a measured 39–41% after three to six years. A brand-name pro lens falls to 3% at year eight against a category average of 67%. The brand rule creates a paradox. Because strong brands decay more slowly, the cliff stands out more against the slow decline, so **strong brands are told to sell sooner**: an iPhone at 2.0 years against 2.17 for a Galaxy, and a Nintendo console at 4.25 years while a generic console, TV or fridge gets "use it up". A Rolex is told to sell at **25.6 years at 44% of price**, a product of a 40-year life cliff that has no basis for watches. Several first-year values are also too generous for durables (a fridge at 0.70 and a Dyson at 0.76, against evidence of 0.45–0.60) and too stingy for luxury goods (a designer bag is treated like clothing, at 0.34).

| App today (computed) | Evidence | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| iPhone value at 1 yr: 0.69 | 0.80–0.87 private; 0.65–0.75 buyback | Matches buyback, not private sale |
| iPhone vs Galaxy gap at 1 yr: 7 points | ~20 points private (2026) | Brand effect too small |
| Over-ear headphones at 4 yrs: 0.06 | ~0.40 | Cliff overrides floor |

The changes are ordered by impact. The first six concern `lifecycle.ts`.

**1. Fix the floor-cliff interaction.** Apply the floor after the cliff, `max(floor, working × cliffFactor)`, or define the cliff's residual as an absolute share of price rather than a share of the working value. As it stands, the floor never binds at end of life.

**2. Make the cliff a per-profile, event-driven option instead of a universal 60%-of-life smoothstep.** Set `cliff: null` for lenses, designer and solid-wood furniture, gold, watches, bags, KitchenAid mixers and manual tools. Use dated triggers where the evidence supports them: iOS or Android support end, Chromebook update expiry, battery health under 80% (with a 10–20% step that resets on a recorded battery replacement), and discontinuation of a camera mount or tool battery platform. Where an age-based onset is still needed, the notes point to about 70–80% of median life rather than 60%.

**3. Replace category curves with sub-category profiles.** The `LIFE_HINTS` regexes already identify phones, laptops, consoles, cameras, vacuums and chairs. Extend each hint so it carries a full curve (`v12`, `perYear`, `floor`, `life`, `cliff`, `g`, `β`) from the first table, and add hints for lenses vs bodies, cordless vs corded vs robot vacuums, pod vs espresso machines, e-bikes, mattresses, IKEA, gold, natural vs lab-grown diamonds, luxury watches and designer bags. Correct the life defaults: microwave about 10 (currently 7), air fryer about 4, cordless vacuum 4.5 (currently 8), pod machine 5, earbuds 3, MacBook 6–7, phones 5 (iPhone 6), camera body 6, lens 12, fridge 12.

**4. Parameterise by value at 12 months.** Store `v12` and derive `firstDrop = 1 − v12/(1 − perYear)`. Most sources report total first-year retention, and the current `firstDrop` hides that `perYear` already applies within year one. Support a linear "points per year" mode for appliances, where the guidance is stated that way.

**5. Replace the flat brand rule (×0.85 on firstDrop, ×0.75 on perYear) with signed multipliers per brand and sub-category.** Use about +15–20 points at year one for iPhone over Galaxy S on private sales, below-average values for Pixel and budget Android, Herman Miller/Knoll ×1.5–2.0, Steelcase ×1.3–1.6, IKEA ×0.6–0.8, Ryobi ×0.75–0.85, and Bosch/Shimano e-bikes ×1.2–1.4. Remove the bonus for Trek, Specialized, Bose and Sony unless calibration supports it, and give Speed Queen no premium. Allow retention of 1.0 or more only for an allowlist (Hermès, Goyard, Rolex steel sports), and only with user confirmation, because every documented appreciation run was followed by a 20–60% correction. Store the multipliers with an "as of" date, since the phone brand gap has moved by 10 points or more between years ([SellCell](https://sellcell.com/blog/samsung-ai-phones-close-resale-gap-iphone-2026/)).

**6. Widen the condition spread and drop the warranty bonus for non-transferable warranties.** Bicycle Blue Book's 38% year-one loss for "excellent" against 62% for "fair" implies fair is worth about 0.6 of excellent, against the app's 0.8 versus 1.1 (about 0.73). Herman Miller and Steelcase warranties do not pass to buyers, so `WARRANTY_BONUS` should be zero for them.

The remaining six concern `upgrade.ts` and `valuation.ts`.

**7. Replace the first-crossing rule with `argmin A(T)` on the monthly grid**, using the formula in the previous section. This removes the systematic three-to-six-month early bias of comparing a forward 12-month loss with a backward average.

**8. Add obsolescence `g·P·t` as the term that justifies selling.** Expose it as "How much do you care about having the latest?" (none, some, a lot), mapped to 0, 1× and 2× the category default. This makes the money-versus-currency trade-off visible instead of buried in a cliff parameter.

**9. Model net proceeds.** Add a fee and channel table keyed by `TaxRegion`, carrying "as of" dates (eBay US 13.6% + $0.40; eBay AU private 0%; FB local 0% and shipped 10%; Swappa about 6.5%; trade-in 0.60–0.75 of private). Add a fixed sale cost for effort, and an ask-to-sale factor of about 0.8. Replace the 12%-of-price `USE_UP_SHARE` test with an absolute net-proceeds threshold (about $30–50 local), so that a $3,000 item at 12% still counts as worth selling and a $60 blender never does.

**10. Benchmark against the replacement, not the sunk past.** Compute `A*` from a fresh item's curve at the expected replacement price (allowing price drift, which is negative for quality-adjusted electronics and positive for consoles and luxury). Fix calibration so the checked price rescales the forward value path without treating the original price as a cost still to be recovered. Today `k` scales `V` but not `P`, which shifts the crossing artificially.

**11. Add expected failure loss** `h(t)·(N(t) + c_f)` to `keepCostNextYear`, and add a "repair or replace" hook. Repair if the cost is under about 50% of the post-repair value, tapering to about 30% at 80% of life, and lower the threshold after a previous major repair ([ServiceMag](https://www.servicemag.org/guides/appliance-repair-vs-replace); [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/finance/when-to-repair-or-replace-your-appliances)).

**12. Present a cost-tolerance window and richer timing tips.** Return `{ start, end }` where `A ≤ 1.05·A*` along with the dollar cost of selling 6 and 12 months early or late. Add statuses for "hold, market-priced". Extend `timingTip` with the Galaxy and Pixel launch calendar, the appliance new-model season, skis, golf, fitness in January, cars in spring, luxury after price rises, and AU month flipping. The current tip for bikes in the US ("late summer and early autumn") conflicts with the March–July peak in the sources ([AOL](https://www.aol.com/month-month-guide-selling-stuff-140000914.html)).

## Conclusion

The most important finding is that the app's best-supported output, a date, rests on its least-supported input, the cliff. Fixing the curves without adding obsolescence would make the planner worse: accurate depreciation-only curves say "never sell", which is right only for an owner who doesn't care about having a current version. So the upgrade planner's real job is to price a trade-off people already make intuitively, money against staying current. Obsolescence should be a visible, user-tunable input rather than a hidden constant. The flatness of the cost curve is good news for that design. Because being months off costs little, the app can be honest about wide uncertainty and still give useful advice. It can save its precision for the moments that move prices: launch announcements, battery thresholds, end of software support and the end of a season.

The largest evidence gaps sit exactly where the app's users have the most money: multi-year curves for laptops, TVs, appliances and premium furniture all rest on rules of thumb or platform marketing. The cheapest way to close them is the app's own data. Each checked price, recorded sale price and battery replacement is a labelled observation. With an "as of" date, these observations could update the category priors over time, so that Keep Track's curves come from its users' own sales data rather than from resale platforms with a commercial interest in the numbers.
